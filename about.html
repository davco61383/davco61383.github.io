About Elementarium

Elementarium is a free, interactive, color-coded periodic table made by David Shier. It runs in any modern web browser on a phone, tablet, or computer, with no account and no download. Its tagline sums it up: everything you can touch has a seat at this table.

What you can do

  • Browse all 118 elements. Each tile shows the atomic number, symbol, name, and atomic weight, colored by chemical family. Tap a family swatch to isolate it.
  • Switch to states of matter. Recolor the table by whether each element is a solid, liquid, or gas at room temperature. Only bromine and mercury are liquids.
  • Open any element. A detail panel shows photographs, atomic weight, group, period, block, phase, appearance, electron configuration, electronegativity, density, melting and boiling points, who discovered it and when, and a couple of “did you know” facts, with a link to read more on Wikipedia.
  • Search by name, symbol, or atomic number, or press Surprise me to land on a random element.
  • Combine elements. Pick two or three elements to see whether they form an ionic compound, a covalent compound, or an alloy, and what common compound they make.
  • Learn the ideas behind the table with short illustrated lessons: how to read chemical bonds, the types of bonds, subatomic particles, and the history of the periodic table, plus a “Test me” quiz.
  • Make it yours. Choose a background from solid colors, metals, birthstone gemstones, patterns, and seasonal themes, change the tile style, and turn background music on or off.
  • Use it in your language. The interface is available in 31 languages, including English, Español, Français, Deutsch, Português, 中文, 日本語, 한국어, हिन्दी, العربية, and Русский.

Read the elements as text

Prefer to read? The Elements guide lists all 118 elements in one table with a written profile of each one, using the same data as the app.

About the data

Atomic weights and temperatures are rounded textbook figures. A bracketed mass, such as [294] for oganesson, is the mass number of the element’s longest-lived isotope, because heavy synthetic elements have no stable form to weigh. Element photographs are drawn from Wikimedia Commons and Wikipedia.
